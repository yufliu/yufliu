## Provisional rules

### Register

Conversational and warm, the way you'd tell a friend where to eat. Enthusiastic
when the food earns it, direct when it doesn't. Not formal, not food-critic
vocabulary, not performatively casual either.

### Opening

Lead with the **dish or the moment**, not the venue. Yelp already displays the
restaurant name, the city, and the stars above the text — repeating them wastes
the first line, which is the only line most people read.

- Good: "The pork belly bao is the reason to come here."
- Bad: "I recently visited Restaurant X in Los Angeles and..."

### Shape

Two to four short paragraphs, or one paragraph plus a short list when there are
several dishes worth naming separately. Roughly:

1. The hook — the standout, or the honest headline if the rating is low
2. The items — named specifically, with one concrete detail each
3. Service and atmosphere — only if there's something real to say
4. The verdict — would you return, what would you order next time, who to bring

Skip any section with nothing genuine behind it.

### Specificity

This is the load-bearing rule. Concrete beats effusive every time:

- "The broth had actual depth — you could taste the bones" over "amazing broth"
- "$14 for six pieces, which is fair for this part of town" over "great value"
- "Came out in under ten minutes on a Saturday night" over "fast service"

### Things to avoid

- Restaurant-review clichés: *hidden gem, nestled, culinary journey, foodie
  paradise, to die for, melts in your mouth, elevated, curated, must-try*
- Heavy em-dash cadence and tidy tricolons — the tells of machine-written text
- Star-rating restatement ("I'm giving this 5 stars because...") — the stars
  are already displayed
- Hedging stacks ("I think maybe it was possibly a bit...")
- Exclamation points beyond one per review
- Emoji in the review body. They read fine on Instagram and read as spam on
  Yelp — **verify this against real samples**, it's an assumption

### Criticism

Specific, fair, aimed at the food or the process — never at a person. "The
rice was undercooked and they didn't check back" is useful to readers and
survivable for the restaurant. "The server was an idiot" is neither.

### Shout-outs

When a specific person made the meal, name them the way a regular would:
"Ask for Marco, he actually knows the menu." Only if the user supplied the
name — never invent one, and don't add a last name.

## Calibration

Replace the guesswork above with real patterns:

**1. Gather 5–10 real reviews.** Any of these work:

- Yu's Yelp profile page (`yelp.com/user_details?userid=...`) — copy the text
  of recent reviews
- Yelp's **"Thanks for your review"** confirmation emails, which quote the full
  review back — searchable in the mailbox given at intake, with permission
- Instagram captions from [@yumilyeats](https://www.instagram.com/yumilyeats/)
  — a weaker signal, since caption voice and review voice differ, but better
  than nothing

**2. Extract, don't summarize.** For each sample, record:

- Word count, sentence count, average sentence length
- How it opens — dish first? scene first? verdict first?
- Paragraph count and whether lists appear
- Punctuation habits: em-dashes, ellipses, parentheticals, exclamation points
- Emoji: present or absent in review bodies?
- Recurring phrases — every writer reuses a few
- Whether dishes get named with prices, and how
- First-person plural ("we") vs. singular ("I")
- How criticism is phrased in the sub-5-star samples specifically

**3. Rewrite this file** with what the samples actually show, delete the
UNVERIFIED banner, and note the sample size and date at the top.

**4. Sanity-check it.** Take one real review, keep only its intake facts
(restaurant, city, items, stars), regenerate it from those facts against the
new profile, and compare to the original. Gaps between the two are gaps in
the profile — fix them and repeat once.
